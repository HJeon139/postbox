# How a bill becomes a US federal law

- **Idea:** The same text has to pass both chambers, and then the President either signs it, lets it become law by waiting, or vetoes it, which two-thirds of each chamber can override.
- **For:** A high-school civics student.
- **Question:** What has to happen for a bill to become law, and what can stop it?
- **Facts:**
  - A member of Congress introduces a bill, and it goes to a committee, which studies it and may change it ([USA.gov, “How laws are made”](https://www.usa.gov/how-laws-are-made)).
  - The chamber votes; if it passes, the other chamber repeats the process. The two chambers work out any differences and both pass the same version, which then goes to the President ([USA.gov](https://www.usa.gov/how-laws-are-made)).
  - The President signs it, or returns it with objections (a veto). If two-thirds of each chamber vote to pass it again, it becomes law ([US Constitution, Article I, Section 7](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript)).
  - A bill the President doesn’t return within ten days, Sundays excepted, becomes law without a signature, unless Congress has adjourned and prevented its return: then it doesn’t, and that pocket veto can’t be overridden ([Article I, Section 7](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript); [USA.gov](https://www.usa.gov/how-laws-are-made)).
- **Shape:** Process with branches.
- **Must show:** both chambers on the main path; the President’s three outcomes as branches; the override as a path back from the veto; the pocket veto as a dead end.
- **Traps:** sentences inside each step; every step drawn the same size so the decisions don’t stand out; leaving out the ten-day branch.
